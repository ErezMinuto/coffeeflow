# Anchor generation on reality, then verify narrowly

Expert teams do not get reliable, on-brand AI imagery by writing ever-longer prompts. They get it by **limiting what the generator has to invent**. The pattern repeats across Unilever's product digital twins, Coca-Cola's archive-seeded platform, Instacart's PIXEL and Mondelez's in-house tool. Generation is anchored on controlled brand inputs: real product photos, 3D twins, or a brand archive. Scenes come from an approved menu, not from free-form briefs. A model writes the prompt as one reconciled scene description. A vision-model judge answers atomic yes/no questions rather than giving a holistic score. A human makes the final publish decision. For a specialty roastery posting Instagram stories and feed posts, that has four concrete consequences. First, the ~7,500-character layered prompt with "FORBIDDEN" lists and "FINAL OVERRIDE" blocks runs against every vendor's guidance. Second, small Hebrew label text cannot be made reliable by any current generator, and survives only if the real label pixels are kept or re-pasted. Third, pours, hands-on-equipment and liquid shots should be photographed and then edited, not synthesised. Fourth, the QA loop should become a fixed, decomposed checklist with crops, repeated votes and logged human verdicts. No vendor publishes Hebrew label-fidelity numbers, and no study measures Gemini 3.x on hands or floating objects. The roastery's own golden set and bake-offs are therefore the only real source of truth for its specific failures.

## One reconciled scene beats layered rules and override blocks

Every vendor's official guidance converges on the same prompt shape. Google says to "describe the scene, don't just list keywords", and that "a narrative, descriptive paragraph will almost always produce a better, more coherent image" ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/how-to-prompt-gemini-2-5-flash-image-generation-for-the-best-results/)). Its formula is **Subject + Action + Location/context + Composition + Style**, written in photographic language: lens, f-stop, lighting setup and materials such as "a minimalist ceramic coffee mug" ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/ultimate-prompting-guide-for-nano-banana)). OpenAI orders gpt-image-1.5 prompts as "background/scene → subject → key details → constraints" and recommends "short labeled segments" for complex requests ([OpenAI Cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/multimodal/image-gen-1.5-prompting_guide)). Black Forest Labs says FLUX.2 "pays more attention to what comes first", calls **30–80 words** "usually ideal", and supports JSON prompts that it understands "equally well" as prose ([BFL FLUX.2 guide](https://docs.bfl.ai/guides/prompting_guide_flux2)). Structure is not the problem. Labeled segments or JSON both work, provided they describe **one** scene.

The divergence is negation. Google tells users to "use positive framing… 'empty street' instead of 'no cars'" ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/ultimate-prompting-guide-for-nano-banana)). Midjourney lists "please don't add fruit" as a bad prompt ([Midjourney docs](https://docs.midjourney.com/docs/no)). Ideogram warns that "strings can still appear" on a guitar even when listed as something to avoid ([Ideogram docs](https://docs.ideogram.ai/llms-full.txt)). The academic record is starker: DALL·E 3 prompts prohibiting an entity "invariably led to images showing X", with only **12.3% human-judged success on negation prompts** ([Conwell et al., arXiv 2411.17066](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17066v1)). Training captions contain a "near-total absence of explicit negations", and combining negation, counting and spatial constraints produces "severe interference" ([arXiv 2511.10136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10136)). OpenAI is the outlier. It endorses short exclusions like "no watermark" ([OpenAI Cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/multimodal/image-gen-1.5-prompting_guide)), and LLM-backbone models do handle simple ones. On Nano Banana, "Do not include any text or watermarks" worked, but "do not generate a digital illustration" failed ([minimaxir.com](https://minimaxir.com/2025/11/nano-banana-prompts/)). The rule that fits all sources: keep one or two generic exclusions, and never list **scene-plausible objects** (kettle, bag, spoon, hands) by name. Naming them injects exactly the concepts most strongly associated with a coffee scene, which is the case where negation is least reliable.

No vendor documents any precedence semantics. Nothing suggests that a "FINAL OVERRIDE, these rules WIN" block makes the model arbitrate between conflicting layers, and the only ordering evidence (FLUX.2) says **earlier** text gets more weight. Length is not the constraint: Gemini 3.1 Flash Image accepts 131,072 input tokens ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/ultimate-prompting-guide-for-nano-banana)), so ~2k tokens is nowhere near truncation. Max Woolf found Nano Banana followed "each and every rule" on a dense but consistent prompt ([minimaxir.com](https://minimaxir.com/2025/11/nano-banana-prompts/)). The failure mode is **contradiction**, and the likely result of shipping a "surface layer", a brief and an override is a blended compromise scene. The fix is to resolve conflicts upstream, in code or an LLM pre-pass, and send the image model a single reconciled description. Two cheap model-side levers also deserve tests. Gemini 3.1 Flash Image defaults to **minimal thinking**, with "high" available. Google's current docs name Nano Banana 2.1 as the recommended model and Gemini 3 Pro Image "for professional asset production" ([ai.google.dev](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-generation)). Aspect ratio belongs in the API config, never in prose.

Synthesising the official templates gives a brief shape that fits all of them:

| Slot | Example content |
|---|---|
| Purpose | "Instagram story for a specialty coffee roastery" (ratio set in API config) |
| Scene and surface | One sentence: "walnut counter by a window" |
| Hero with support | "the bag stands upright, its base touching the wood, soft shadow to the left" |
| Secondary objects | An exhaustive positive list with counts (three or fewer of each type) and frame positions |
| Hands | Only if needed: "a single right hand holding the kettle by its handle" |
| Camera, light, grade | Angle, lens, f-stop, light source, colour mood |
| Exclusions | At most "no text, no watermark" |
| References | Each image with a named role ("image 1 is the exact bag; keep its label unchanged") |

Explicit contact language matters because spatial relations are a documented weak point. DALL·E 3 agreed on spatial relations only about 45% of the time, and models default to the configurations most common in training ([arXiv 2411.17066](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17066v1)). Counting is "relatively reasonable for 1–3 entities" and drops off at four ([same](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17066v1)).

## Generators redraw labels, so exact Hebrew needs real pixels

Every reference-conditioned model **re-synthesises** the product rather than copying pixels, so fidelity is probabilistic. Short Latin strings now usually survive. The img.ly benchmark gives Nano Banana Pro **5.0/5 on text accuracy** across product-label and wordmark tests. The same model scores only **3.3/5 on product staging** and 3.6/5 on brand colour, at about **$0.15 per image** ([img.ly](https://img.ly/ai-benchmarks/models/nano-banana-pro.md)). Kittl reports that dense text blocks "can introduce occasional letter swaps" ([Kittl](https://www.kittl.com/blogs/kittl-review-nano-banana-pro/)). Small right-to-left fine print is the hardest case, and **no vendor or independent benchmark reports Hebrew label accuracy for any model**. One vendor says Hebrew requests "usually produce gibberish", and advises adding essential copy after generation ([Kolbo.AI](https://kolbo.ai/blog/best-ai-tools-hebrew-support)). A recognisable bag with garbled Hebrew descriptor and address text is therefore expected behaviour, not a prompt bug. Fine-tuning does not rescue it. LoRA guides recommend 15–50 images ([fal](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/flux-2-trainer)), but no source shows LoRA reproducing exact label text, and Vertex subject customization reinventing the label is the typical outcome.

That leaves three production-grade routes, ordered by how much they guarantee:

| Approach | Label fidelity | Realism risk | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Background generation around the real cutout (Bria, Photoroom) | Exact (pixels kept) | "Pasted" look unless shadows and relighting are added | Bria promises "pixel-perfect fidelity" and ships a shadow endpoint ([Bria](https://bria.ai/product-shots)); Photoroom offers AI Shadows and Relighting at **$0.10/image** ([Photoroom](https://www.photoroom.com/inside-photoroom/api-product-updates)) |
| Generate the full scene, then re-inject label detail (frequency separation) | Exact if re-paste or warp succeeds | Curved pouches need mesh warps | IC-Light DetailTransfer re-adds the original's high frequencies to fix garbled logos ([comfy.icu](https://comfy.icu/node/DetailTransfer)); DreamLight's wavelet "Spectral Foreground Fixer" ([arXiv 2506.14549](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.14549)) |
| Reference conditioning with fidelity controls | Probabilistic | Lowest | OpenAI `input_fidelity="high"` helps with "logos, or any other details", but only the **first** input image gets the extra richness ([OpenAI Cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/generate_images_with_high_input_fidelity)) |

The earlier "PNG paste looked fake" failure is consistent with the research. Realism comes from **low-frequency lighting transfer plus a contact shadow**, not from raw pixels. Hi-Light warns that copying high-frequency lightness directly causes ghosting ([arXiv 2601.23167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.23167)). The buildable hybrid for a small team keeps Gemini's coherent scene, detects the bag, warps the true label region onto it, blends with the generated image's lighting, and then runs a text check. That check needs care: OCR models "degrade substantially" on mid-resource scripts ([GlotOCR Bench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.12978)), so validate the Hebrew OCR gate against the real bag photo before trusting it. Amazon's case for doing this at all is commercial. It reports CTR "up to 40% higher" for products with lifestyle context than on plain white ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2023/10/25/amazons-new-ai-image-tool-puts-fake-backgrounds-behind-real-products)).

## Physics fails where pours, hands and contact meet

The roastery's failures (a floating V60, a portafilter pouring with no machine, a kettle swapped for a milk pitcher, two left hands) each map onto a documented benchmark category. PhyBench found models "struggle to understand physical scenarios such as gravity and buoyancy", "largely fail" on material properties, and do well only on optics ([arXiv 2406.11802](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11802v3)). PicWorld's 17 models "universally exhibit a fundamental limitation" in physical causal reasoning ([arXiv 2511.18271](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18271v2)). HandEval documents frequent extra or missing fingers even when overall quality is high ([arXiv 2510.08978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08978)). Hand-fixing inpainters work only where "the hand is not interacting nor occluded by other objects" ([GraspDiffusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.13911v3)), which is exactly the barista case. Food-and-beverage practitioners name pours, steam, clear vessels and condensation as the riskiest subjects, and recommend "a real hero capture when the motion must be exact" ([Nightjar](https://nightjar.so/blog/food-beverage-product-photography-with-ai)). Specialist equipment such as a Strada X or a gooseneck kettle on a V60 stand is plausibly sparse in training data. That is an inference: no source tests coffee gear.

The most reliable mitigation is to **start from a real photo, where the physics is already correct**, and make narrow edits. Google's editing template says "change only the [element]… Keep everything else in the image exactly the same" ([ai.google.dev](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-generation)). Kontext guidance is to change one attribute per request, background first, and to avoid drastic lighting shifts ([Comfy docs](https://docs.comfy.org/ko/tutorials/partner-nodes/black-forest-labs/flux-1-kontext)). Artifacts appear after about **six turns** ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/ai-gateway/models/flux-kontext-pro)), and single-edit identity "does not necessarily translate into stable identity over a longer editing workflow" ([arXiv 2609.04151](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04151)). Cap edit chains, and retry from the original rather than from a failed edit. Reframing 4:3 to 9:16 should be its own outpaint step, because expansion keeps the original pixel budget and softens the result ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/bug-reports-403/useless-expand-in-firefly-1485986)). Among editors, CREval ranks Seedream 4.0 and Qwen-Image-Edit at the top. It attributes GPT-Image-1's weaker showing to "poor consistency of key visual elements" ([arXiv 2603.26174](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26174)).

When generation is unavoidable, three things help. Spelling out the physics in the prompt measurably improved PhyBench scores ([arXiv 2406.11802](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11802v3)). Structure-first methods lay out the grasp before rendering appearance ([Affordance Diffusion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.12538)). Composing around **"the moment after the action"** helps too: a filled cup on the drip tray, or a dripper resting on its stand. The large-brand programmes design out failure categories rather than trying to QA them away. Mondelez does not generate human likenesses ([ESM Magazine](https://www.esmmagazine.com/technology/oreo-maker-mondelez-to-use-new-generative-ai-tool-to-slash-marketing-costs-298596)), and Unilever's digital twins hold "all product variants, labels, packaging and language formats in a single file" ([Campaign](https://campaignlive.com/article/unilever-uses-digital-twins-accelerate-content-creation/1911080)). The roastery analogue is a **shoot-once asset library** of pours, extractions, latte art, hands on the machine and every bag, which AI then reframes, relights and restyles. This mirrors Coca-Cola's use of archive assets as the generative base ([WARC](https://www.warc.com/content/article/coca-cola-create-real-magic/en-gb/156127)).

## Vision judges need atomic questions, crops and votes

MLLM judges agree with humans well enough on pairwise choices (**79.3%** for GPT-4V) but poorly on absolute scores (**Pearson 0.49**), and absolute scoring is the least self-consistent format across repeated runs ([MLLM-as-a-Judge, arXiv 2402.04788](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.04788v3)). Their blind spots line up with the roastery's failures. On trivial tasks such as whether two circles overlap or how many shapes there are, frontier VLMs averaged **58.57%**, and the best reached 74% ([BlindTest, arXiv 2407.06581](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06581v4)). Anthropic's own documentation says Claude's spatial outputs "are approximate", counts "might not always be precisely accurate", and precision tasks need "human oversight" ([Claude Vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). Temperature 0 is "necessary but not sufficient". It cut verdict flips by only 43–79% depending on the model ([arXiv 2606.13685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13685)), and residual noise comes from server-side batching ([arXiv 2503.09347](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.09347)). A floating dripper that passes on some runs and fails on others is the expected behaviour of a borderline contact judgment, not a bug in the judge prompt. Biases compound the noise. Swapping display order reversed up to **60.9%** of pairwise editing verdicts ([arXiv 2610.01670](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01670)), and models tend to favour their own family's outputs ([arXiv 2604.11589](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11589)). That makes a Gemini judge for Gemini images a risk worth removing, although no study measures self-preference on images specifically.

What works is narrower than "think harder". Generic chain-of-thought **lowered** GPT-4V's scoring agreement with humans from 0.557 to 0.299. Adding a describe-first step raised it from 0.299 to 0.435 ([arXiv 2402.04788](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.04788v3)). Decomposed yes/no questions track humans better than holistic Likert scores ([Gecko](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16820)). Davidsonian Scene Graph makes those questions atomic and dependency-ordered: there is no point asking about handedness if no hand is present ([DSG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18235)). VQAScore-ranked best-of-N improved human ratings **2–3× more** than PickScore or ImageReward did ([GenAI-Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13743)). Models often attend to the right region but misperceive it, so crops and zoom directly improve detail checks ([ViCrop](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.17422)). Forcing per-hand enumeration fixed "two left hands" because it turned a holistic impression into a targeted perceptual question. Judge calls are cheap: about **$5 per 1,000** one-megapixel images on Opus 5.5 input pricing ([Claude Vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). Three votes over the full frame plus crops of the hands, the contact point and the label are therefore affordable.

The best-documented production loop, Instacart's PIXEL, has an LLM write yes/no evaluation questions per project. A VLM answers them, the failed questions are **fed back to rewrite the prompt**, and humans stay in the loop. It lifted human approval of AI images from **20% to 85%** ([Instacart PIXEL](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/introducing-pixel-instacarts-unified-image-generation-platform)). Generic preference reward models are not a substitute for brand-rule checks. On an independent human-ranked set, all of them scored below 0.5 pairwise agreement ([arXiv 2604.11490](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11490)). The judge should be a high-recall filter for objective failures: wrong product, missing bag, extra limbs, text errors. Images where repeated votes disagree, and any category in a documented blind spot without a unanimous crop check, should route to the human gate. Meta's own ad terms put review on the advertiser, after AI tools produced a two-handlebar bike and dresses turned into trousers ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/meta-ai-ad-tools-brands-chaos)).

## Approved scene menus outperform free-form agent briefs

No public case study shows an LLM agent writing free-form scene briefs straight into a generator in production. Brands constrain the **request**, not just the output. Coca-Cola routes every use through "a very simple form and our small team looks at it" and keeps an approved-tools list ([Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/what-coca-cola-learned-generative-ai/741709/)). Adobe's Firefly Design Intelligence encodes brand rules for compliance ([Constellation Research](https://www.constellationr.com/insights/news/adobe-expand-genstudio-launches-custom-firefly-models-firefly-foundry)). Typeface learns image styles from 10–15 on-theme examples ([Maginative](https://www.maginative.com/article/typeface-unveils-multimodal-brand-kit-to-ensure-consistency-in-ai-generated-content/)). BannerAgency keeps brand and layout elements as editable vectors and uses the image model only for backgrounds ([ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.emnlp-main.214)). Prompt instructions alone are not enough. YouTube's ads team traced an agent removing legal disclaimers "despite repeated instructions not to" ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/building-production-grade-evaluations-for-llm-based-agents-at-scale)). Constraints that matter should be structural, enforced by a schema or enum, not left as a request in prose.

For a strategist agent feeding an image pipeline, the small-scale analogue is a **shot library**. Each entry is a scene type the model has been shown to render reliably, with its reference images, a fixed reconciled prompt skeleton, and its own QA checklist. The agent fills only typed slots: product, mood, caption angle, season. A feasibility filter rejects briefs that require unsupported physics, such as hands in contact, motion pours or transparent liquids, unless the entry is backed by a real photo. A text-image alignment check runs before approval. Variety can be managed with a rotation ledger over scene × product × surface × angle with cooldowns. That control is inferred; no brand has published an anti-repetition system. The menu also handles the authenticity risk. NIQ's EEG study found consumers describe AI ads as "annoying, boring, and confusing" ([ESM Magazine](https://esmmagazine.com/a-brands/ai-generated-ads-may-have-a-negative-effect-on-consumer-perceptions-279327)). A controlled study found significantly lower trust in AI ads (d = 0.89) ([Commusty](https://journal.undiknas.ac.id/index.php/commusty/article/view/7734)). A San Francisco coffee shop faced graffiti backlash over AI food imagery ([NBC Bay Area](https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/tech/ai-food-images-jump-from-online-to-irl-menus/4136326/?amp=1)). J.Crew's damage came as much from undisclosed AI as from a backwards-pointing ankle ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2025/09/02/j-crews-ai-generated-fashion-images-look-a-lot-like-real-photos)). For a craft roastery, menu entries that depict the product and process should be real-photo-based by default.

## Golden sets and logged verdicts make changes measurable

The most concrete public evaluation practice comes from YouTube's ads team ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/building-production-grade-evaluations-for-llm-based-agents-at-scale)). Inspect informally first, because scaling too early made metrics volatile. Then build a golden set with strong human-human agreement and **several examples per failure pattern**. Score dimensions separately, with written rationales. Run side-by-side comparisons between model and prompt versions. Sample LLM-judge verdicts against expert ratings and track the disagreement. Set launch thresholds before running the comparison. Academic work adds three cautions. Human raters themselves only reach Krippendorff's α ≈ 0.72 ([GenAI-Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13743)). Prompt sets should be organised by skill so failures can be localised ([Gecko](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16820)). Judges must be tested for invariance and stability, not just human agreement ([arXiv 2610.01670](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01670)). Pre-testing has limits too: Coca-Cola's holiday spot passed tests for "acceptability, understanding and entertainment value" and still drew backlash ([Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/what-coca-cola-learned-generative-ai/741709/)).

For a three-person team this reduces to a small, disciplined harness. The owner's rejection reasons are already a rubric: off-brand, not logical or believable, text-image mismatch, repetitive, and wrong label. Log every approve or reject with its reason, and compute acceptance rate by scene type, prompt version and model. That is the online metric. Freeze a golden set of past briefs, both accepted and rejected, spanning every shot-library entry. Re-run it as a **regression test** on every prompt, model or judge change before deploying. The judge's precision and recall against the owner's historical verdicts decide which checks may auto-reject. Bake-offs are the only way to answer the open questions the research could not:

- Nano Banana 2.1 vs Gemini 3 Pro Image vs high thinking level.
- Gemini vs GPT-image with `input_fidelity` on the Hebrew label.
- Bria or Photoroom background generation vs label re-paste.

Run each bake-off on fixed briefs, generate several candidates per arm, and judge pairwise in both display orders, keeping only order-consistent verdicts. Vendor cost and conversion claims, such as Klarna's $6M image "saving", which Digiday showed equalled its entire former budget ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/marketing/how-klarna-is-using-ai-for-cost-savings-changing-extremely-frustrating-creative-processes/)), are no substitute for this in-house evidence.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the roastery's problem. The failures do not come from an under-specified prompt that a stricter rule or a louder override could fix. They come from asking a generator to invent things it demonstrably cannot (exact Hebrew glyphs, contact physics, hands on specialist equipment) and then asking a judge to verify things it demonstrably cannot see reliably (contact, counts, fine text). The leverage therefore sits at the two ends of the pipeline rather than in the middle. Upstream, shrink what must be invented: real-photo anchors, label pixels that are never redrawn, and a scene menu that excludes unsupported physics. Downstream, shrink what must be judged at once: atomic questions, crops of the risky regions, and repeated votes. Each fix also makes the next one easier. A menu entry has a known failure profile, so its checklist can be short and specific, and its golden examples double as regression tests.

The less obvious implication is that the human approval click is the pipeline's most valuable data source, not just a safety gate. Every logged verdict with a reason calibrates the judge, ranks the shot-library entries by real acceptance rate, and turns model or prompt upgrades from guesswork into measured bake-offs. Teams that skip this logging end up re-arguing the same aesthetic disagreements with each model release. Teams that keep it can let the automated judge auto-reject more categories over time, and keep the owner's attention for the decisions only a person with taste can make.
